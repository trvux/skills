# Case studies: how the process looks worked through for real products

These are original analyses of the *reasoning* behind a few well-known
products' design identities — for showing the user how step 2-3 of the
process plays out, not as source material to copy tokens or copy from.
Use these to illustrate the shape of a good point-of-view sentence, then
push the user to write their own from their own audience.

## Linear (project management for software teams)

**Audience state**: engineers and PMs who already find most PM tools slow
and click-heavy, and resent the time cost of "process."

**Point of view**: "This should feel as fast as the keyboard-driven editor
you already live in, because our users' actual complaint about every
competitor is friction, not missing features."

**Signature**: the command palette / keyboard-first interaction model
itself — not a visual device at all. The dark theme and violet accent are
downstream of "feels like a tool for people who live in dark-themed
editors," not the point of the design.

**What this teaches**: the signature doesn't have to be decorative. For a
product whose whole pitch is speed, the signature can be an interaction
pattern. A visual identity built to *prove* that pattern (minimal chrome,
no modal-heavy flows, instant transitions) reads as coherent because every
choice traces back to the same sentence.

## Framer (visual site builder with real motion/interaction tooling)

**Audience state**: designers deciding between a design tool and this,
who need to believe the output can be genuinely interactive, not just a
static mockup exported to HTML.

**Point of view**: "The marketing site itself has to move, because the
product's entire value proposition is motion the audience can't get
anywhere else — a static site would undersell the product by definition."

**Signature**: real, layered motion (scroll-driven, cursor-reactive,
physics-based) used throughout, not just in one hero moment.

**What this teaches**: sometimes the signature is allowed to be
everywhere, if the point of view is specifically about a capability the
whole product has to demonstrate. This is the exception to "spend
boldness in one place" — it's still disciplined, because every animation
serves the same one claim (this tool does motion well), not five
unrelated flourishes.

## shadcn/ui (a source-you-own component collection, not a hosted library)

**Audience state**: developers who are going to put their *own* brand on
top of this, and will resent anything opinionated enough to fight that.

**Point of view**: "This should feel like it has no opinions of its own,
because our users' brand is supposed to be the visible one, not ours."

**Signature**: the absence of a strong visual signature is itself the
choice — neutral grayscale defaults, restrained radius, no distinctive
color unless the consumer sets one. Distinctiveness here would be a bug,
not a feature.

**What this teaches**: "no visible personality" is a legitimate, disciplined
answer to the point-of-view question — for a product whose job is to get
out of the way. Copying shadcn's neutral palette onto a consumer-facing
marketing site without asking whether *that* product also wants to
disappear is a common, avoidable mistake.

## Stripe (payments infrastructure, developer- and finance-facing)

**Audience state**: two audiences at once — engineers deciding whether the
API is good, and finance/exec stakeholders deciding whether the company is
trustworthy enough to hold their money.

**Point of view**: "This should feel simultaneously like serious financial
infrastructure and like a well-made developer tool, because either
audience walking away unconvinced kills the deal."

**Signature**: precise, almost print-like typography and layout grid,
paired with occasional playful gradient/illustration moments used
sparingly — rigor as the dominant note, warmth as the accent, matching a
product that has to be trusted with money but built by people, not a bank.

**What this teaches**: a point of view can legitimately serve two
audiences at once, but only if the designer is explicit about which one is
dominant. Trying to weight them equally usually produces something that
satisfies neither.

## Using these with a real intake

When running step 2-3 with a user, walk through one of these that's
closest to their situation and ask "what's the equivalent sentence for
*your* audience?" rather than "which of these do you want to look like."
The goal is transferring the reasoning pattern, not the aesthetic.
